The Deposit

One

Keep functions, compost forms

When a practice depends on conditions someone else holds — a platform, a sponsor, a schedule, a room — the form dies when the conditions do. The function doesn't have to. Before adopting any form, name the function it serves; then ask whether that function can be re-embedded in what you already control. Compost forms freely. Keep functions. A practice rebuilt on your own ground is smaller and cannot be taken.

Two

What a framework makes visible

A framework does not describe reality; it restructures what its user can perceive. So the question to ask of one is not "is it true?" but "what does it make visible that was invisible — and what has it stopped me from seeing?" Run the test after a month inside any framework: name one thing you now see that you couldn't before, and one thing you no longer notice. If you can't answer the first, drop it. If you can't answer the second, look harder — there is always a second answer.

Three

The cooling is part of the work

Transmission has real cost, and the cost is metabolized by the transmitter. Sending the hard thing — the report, the disclosure, the honest letter — does not discharge it; the one who carries the message heats up, and the heat takes time to leave the body. Budget for this. The send is not the end of the work. The cooling is part of the work, and no one else can do it for you.

Four

Coherence reorganizes its surroundings

Coherence reorganizes its surroundings. A structure that holds its shape, placed in a field that doesn't, does not sit neutrally — the field moves. People and institutions rearrange around whatever stays coherent near them, without anyone deciding to. Consequence: you rarely need to push. You need to hold shape and remain. Expect resistance proportional to the differential, and read the resistance as confirmation of the pressure, not refutation of the shape.

Five

The two organs

Any personal system that another person might one day join needs two organs. A private organ, illegible by design — where the raw living happens and no reader is owed anything. A public organ — where the system becomes joinable, and everything present is there to be read. The one-organ failures: make everything legible and the system becomes a performance; make everything private and it can never be joined. The discipline is to decide organ by organ, never line by line, and to let the private organ stay genuinely unreadable without apology.

Six

The pattern leaves, the instance stays

What transfers between people, systems, and containers is the pattern — the shape of the move. The instance — the case, the names, the wound, the particulars — stays with its owner. Design every handoff so the pattern can leave and the instance cannot. This is a privacy law and a transmission law in one sentence: strip the instance and the pattern travels farther; leak the instance and the pattern arrives addressed, which is to say, it arrives owned.

Seven

The stall that costs one sentence

Some stalled decisions have no remaining work-cost. The labor is covered, the materials are ready, the path is scoped — and the thing still doesn't move. The stall reads as a resource problem and is actually a recognition problem: nobody has said out loud that a decision is what's missing. The move: itemize the real remaining costs. If the list comes back empty, the price of motion is a single recognition — so name it, and name who can pay it. It can be done by tomorrow at the cost of one sentence.

Eight

The availability audit

A channel of support exists; the person who needs it cannot afford its price — in capacity, exposure, standing, or energy. Nearly every system audits existence ("we have a process for that") and almost none audit availability. The two-line audit: does the path exist, and can the person who needs it most pay what using it costs? If the price exceeds their capacity, then for them the support does not exist — and the system's belief that it does is the most dangerous thing in the building.

Nine

The gap between stacked containers

Between stacked containers — a sponsor and its project, a parent organization and its program, a platform and its community — runs an unexamined relation: each assumes the other holds what neither holds. The duty of care, the records, the person in crisis — each layer believes it belongs to the other layer. The gap is invisible from inside either container and total from inside the fall. The audit: for each thing a participant might one day need, name which container holds it. Anything both containers point away from, neither holds — and someone is standing on it.

Ten

Every surface selects a population

Every surface a container presents selects a population. The forms, the tone, the hours, the language, the price of entry — each face admits some people and turns the rest away before anyone decides anything. A container's actual population is the output of its filters, not of its intent. To change who arrives, change the face. And know that an unreachable face is not a slow filter — it selects nobody, perfectly.

Eleven

Two kinds of labor

Two kinds of labor. Work that closes a loop: the task ends and the pile is smaller. Work that opens the field: the task ends and there are more possible moves than before — water where there wasn't, a cleared pad, a tool that stays. Both are real. But a place that asks its people only for loop-closing burns them, and a place that gets some field-opening work compounds. The sorting question for any task: when this is done, are there more possible moves here, or fewer?

Twelve

Presence without investment

A complete gift in one sentence: sit with someone while they sort what to keep, as a person with no stake in the outcome. The gift is somatic safety against decision fatigue — not advice, not labor, not opinion. Presence without investment. It costs an afternoon, requires no skill, and asks only this discipline: care about the person completely and about the outcome not at all.

Thirteen

The gift move works on institutions

The gift move works on institutions. Give one exactly what its own framework obligates it to want — the documentation, the lens, the clean path to compliance — and ask nothing back. An institution knows how to discharge a demand: pay it, fight it, wait it out. It has no mechanism for discharging a gift. The obligation with no counterparty circulates inside it, changing decisions made by people who will never know where it came from. The pattern propagates. The source disappears.

Fourteen

Carrying is not asking

Transporting someone's material — their words, their need, their document — is not the same act as making their ask for them. An intermediary who lets carrying quietly become asking has forged a request. The discipline: deliver what was given, unaltered; if an ask is needed, it comes from its owner or it does not come. This is one law for advocates, translators, assistants, and every system that moves other people's words.

Fifteen

Every counting pass declares what it added

Any audit that writes to the ledger it audits changes the count. There is no choice about whether — only about whether it says so. A self-measuring system that reports its own marks stays honest; one that hides them is off by exactly itself. The rule fits in a line: every counting pass ends by declaring what it added.

Sixteen

The empty chair, kept visible

A system that admits what it does not yet have — a seat named, labeled, and held open — is stronger than one that decorates the gap. Vacancy stated is design honesty. Vacancy papered over is debt, accruing quietly until the missing thing is needed. Keep the empty chair visible and empty until the real occupant arrives; the chair filled with a mockup is the only truly empty one.

Seventeen

Would its own operator stand at it daily?

Every instrument of self-governance — the dashboard, the ritual, the review — gets one test: would its own operator stand at it daily? Not admire it, not defend it. Stand at it. Daily governance has to run at the speed of honest typing; an instrument that requires more capacity than the moves it governs has become extraction, however correct its architecture. Write the falsifier down where you can see it. Then answer it.

Eighteen

The cost you built nothing for

A long-running system converts its costs into structure — every recurring pain eventually gets a tool. Except one. Every such system has one class of cost it names precisely, repeatedly, eloquently — and builds nothing for. The naming produces commentary, boundaries, philosophy about the cost; the tool never comes. The audit: list your instruments and the cost each one metabolizes. The cost you have written about most and built for least is the one. This piece does not say what to build. It says the seat is empty, and that the emptiness is visible from outside.

Nineteen

The map that arbitrates nothing

Map someone's entire option space — every fork live, fruit hanging on the branches, real costs named — and hand the map over, arbitrating nothing. The gift is not a recommendation. It is the field itself, with the choosing left wholly theirs, because receiving and deciding are separate powers and the map must not collapse them. The discipline: no fork closed, no route ranked, nothing scheduled. The map is complete when its maker's preference cannot be recovered from it.

Twenty

The whole test of membership

The class all of this belongs to: an artifact carrying value with no name that must travel with it — no author to credit, no source to negotiate with, no return path to close. It reaches whoever picks it up, and it can be passed on by a stranger, changed or unchanged, with nothing outstanding in either direction. That is the whole test of membership. Built right, the pattern propagates and the source disappears.
